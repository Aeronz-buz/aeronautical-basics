Stresses in Beams

A beam turns applied loads into internal shear and bending — and every structure that spans a gap, from a bridge girder to an aircraft wing, is designed around exactly this behaviour. This first part works through beam types, load types, and shear force & bending moment diagrams for seven worked problems. Why Beams Matter

What is a beam? A structural member that is long compared with its lateral dimensions, subjected to transverse forces that bend it in an axial plane. Floor joists, bridge girders, aircraft spars and shelf brackets are all beams.

Why study stresses in beams? Every beam in service must survive the loads placed on it without excessive deflection or failure. Before a beam can be sized, an engineer must know exactly how much internal stress and strain those loads create at every cross-section along its length.

The objective. When a beam is loaded by forces or couples, stresses and strains are created throughout its interior. To determine these, the internal forces and internal couples acting on every cross-section of the beam must first be found — this is the starting point for everything else in this unit: shear force and bending moment diagrams, the theory of bending, and shear stress distribution.

The Chain of Analysis
Loads → Reactions → SF & BM → Bending & Shear Stress
Every beam problem in this unit follows this same sequence, no matter how complex the loading.
01

Beam Types & Load Types

Beams are classified by how they are supported. Support conditions decide how many reactions there are, whether the beam is statically determinate, and what shape the bending moment diagram takes.

Types of Beams

(a) Cantilever

Fixed at one end and free at the other. The built-in support resists both the load and the rotation, so all the shear and bending is carried at the wall. This is the working principle of a diving board or an aircraft wing.

Supports
One fixed end, one free end
Analysis
Statically determinate

(b) Simply supported

Rests on a pin at one end and a roller at the other, so it is free to rotate at both ends. The reactions follow from equilibrium alone. This is the most common configuration in floors and bridges.

Supports
Pin at one end, roller at the other
Analysis
Statically determinate

(c) Overhanging

Supported at two points, but one or both ends extend beyond a support. The overhang produces hogging moment over the support, so the beam combines simple-beam and cantilever behaviour.

Supports
Two supports, ends project past them
Analysis
Statically determinate

(d) Continuous

Supported at more than two points and spanning several bays. Common in long bridges and multi-span floors, where the moment over the interior supports relieves the moment at mid-span.

Supports
Three or more supports
Analysis
Statically indeterminate

(e) Fixed-ended

Both ends are rigidly built in, so rotation is prevented at each end. It is stiffer than a simply supported beam of the same span and material, with smaller deflection and smaller mid-span moment.

Supports
Fixed at both ends
Analysis
Statically indeterminate

(f) Propped cantilever

A cantilever whose free end is also held up by a roller prop. The prop shares the load with the fixed support, which reduces both the deflection and the moment at the wall.

Supports
Fixed end plus a roller prop at the free end
Analysis
Statically indeterminate

Load Types on Beams

Alongside support conditions, the shape of the loading controls the shear and moment diagrams. Four types cover almost every real case:

Point load

A force acting at a single point on the beam, such as a column bearing on a girder. Here the weights W1 and W2 hang from the beam through hangers.

Unit
N or kN
Resultant
The load itself, at its point of application

Uniformly distributed load (UDL)

A load spread evenly along the span, given as force per unit length (q or w). The self-weight of a uniform beam is the usual example.

Unit
kN/m
Resultant
w × L, acting at mid-length

Uniformly varying load (UVL)

A distributed load that grows (or falls) linearly along the length, from zero at one end to a maximum intensity w at the other. Hydrostatic pressure on a retaining wall is the classic case.

Unit
kN/m (peak intensity w)
Resultant
½ × w × L, acting one-third of the length from the larger end

Concentrated moment

A pure couple applied at a point. Here two weights W pass over pulleys and pull the ends of a rigid bracket in opposite directions, so the beam is twisted locally with no net transverse force.

Unit
kN·m
Resultant
None: a couple has no net force
02

Shear Force & Bending Moment

When a beam is cut at any cross-section, the resultant of the internal stresses on that section must maintain equilibrium of the free body on either side. That resultant reduces to just two quantities: a shear force and a bending moment. In statically determinate beams, both can be found directly from the equations of equilibrium.

Shear Force, F
At any x-section, the shear force is the algebraic sum of all the lateral components of the forces acting on either side of that section.
Bending Moment, M
The bending moment at an x-section is the algebraic sum of the moments, about that section, of all the forces acting on either side of it.

Sign Convention

A shear force is positive when the left portion tends to move up relative to the right. A bending moment is positive when it causes the beam to sag (concave upward, tension in the bottom fibre) and negative when it causes hogging (concave downward, tension in the top fibre).

03

Shear Force & Bending Moment Diagrams

Seven worked examples span the two support conditions (cantilever, simply supported) crossed with the main load types (point load, UDL, UVL, and a mixed combination). Switch between them below — each shows the loading diagram, the full working, and the final SFD/BMD.

Problem 1 — Cantilever with a point load at the free end

A cantilever of length L carries a concentrated load P at its free end. Draw the SFD and BMD.

Working
  1. Take a section at distance x from the free end B.
  2. Shear force is constant along the span: SFx = −P.
  3. Bending moment grows linearly with x: BMx = −P·x.
  4. At x = 0 (free end): BMB = 0. At x = L (fixed end): BMA = −P·L.

The SFD is a rectangle of constant magnitude P; the BMD is a straight triangle peaking at −PL over the fixed support — the point every cantilever must be checked for maximum bending stress.

Problem 2 — Cantilever with a uniformly distributed load

A cantilever beam is subjected to a uniformly distributed load (UDL) of intensity w over its full length. Draw the SFD and BMD.

Working
  1. At distance x from the free end, the load acting is w·x.
  2. Shear force: SFx = −w·x — varies linearly, zero at the tip and maximum at the support.
  3. Bending moment: BMx = −(w·x)·x/2 = −w·x²/2 — a parabolic curve.
  4. At x = 0: BMB = 0. At x = L: BMA = −w·L²/2, and SFA = −w·L.

Because the load builds up along the length, the SFD is a straight sloped line while the BMD curves as a parabola — a good example of the dV/dx = −w relationship in action.

Problem 3 — Cantilever with a uniformly varying load

A cantilever beam carries a uniformly varying load (UVL), zero at the free end and maximum (w) at the fixed end. Draw the SFD and BMD.

Working
  1. The load intensity at distance x from the tip is (w·x)/L.
  2. Total load on the segment 0→x is the area of the small triangle: ½ · x · (w·x/L) = w·x²/(2L).
  3. Shear force: SFx = −w·x²/(2L) — a parabolic curve.
  4. Bending moment: BMx = −[w·x²/(2L)]·(x/3) = −w·x³/(6L) — a cubic curve.
  5. At x = L: SFA = −w·L/2 and BMA = −w·L²/6.

Each load type steps up the curve order by one: point load → straight BMD, UDL → parabolic BMD, UVL → cubic BMD. That pattern is worth memorising.

Problem 9 — Simply supported beam, point load at mid-span

A simply supported beam of length L carries a concentrated load P at its mid-span. Draw the SFD and BMD.

Working
  1. Reactions by symmetry: RA = RC = P/2.
  2. From A to the load point B: SF = −P/2 (constant, negative by the usual sign convention on this side).
  3. From B to C: SF = +P/2 — the shear force jumps by P at the load point.
  4. Bending moment rises linearly from zero at each support to a maximum of P·L/4 directly under the load.

The jump in the SFD exactly equals the applied point load — a useful check on any shear diagram. Maximum bending moment occurs where the SFD crosses zero.

Problem 10 — Simply supported beam, point load at distance a from support

A simply supported beam of length L carries a concentrated load P at a distance a from the left support (b = L − a from the right).

Working
  1. Taking moments about A: RC·L = P·a → RC = P·a/L.
  2. From vertical equilibrium: RA = P·b/L.
  3. SF is −P·b/L from A to the load, then jumps to +P·a/L from the load to C.
  4. Maximum bending moment occurs under the load: BMmax = P·a·b/L.

When a = b = L/2 this reduces exactly to Problem 9 — a good consistency check on the general eccentric-load formula.

Problem 11 — Simply supported beam under a full-span UDL

A simply supported beam of length L carries a uniformly distributed load of intensity W per unit length over its entire span.

Working
  1. By symmetry, RA = RB = W·L/2.
  2. Shear force varies linearly from +WL/2 at A to −WL/2 at B, crossing zero at mid-span.
  3. Bending moment is a parabola, maximum at mid-span: BMmax = W·L²/8.
  4. This W·L²/8 result is one of the most-used constants in beam design.

Because the SFD crosses zero exactly at the centre, the maximum moment for a symmetric UDL case always falls at mid-span.

Problem 13 — Simply supported beam under a mix of point loads and a UDL

A simply supported, 8 m beam carries a 50 kN point load 2 m from A, a 20 kN/m UDL over the next 2 m, and a 100 kN point load 2 m further along, with the last 2 m unloaded to support B.

Working
  1. Reactions from equilibrium: ΣV = 0 gives RA + RB = 190 kN; taking moments about A gives RB = 112.5 kN, so RA = 77.5 kN.
  2. Shear is built up support-reaction by support-reaction, load by load: it steps at each point load and slopes through the UDL segment.
  3. The shear force crosses zero at x = 2.625 m into the UDL zone — that is where bending moment peaks.
  4. Bending moment is calculated at every load point and at the zero-shear location, giving a maximum of 228.9 kN·m.

Combination-loading problems like this are solved the same way as the simple cases — just applied piece by piece, load by load, along the span. This single diagram carries the full loading, SFD and BMD together.

How materials carry load, and where they give way.

Mechanics of materials connects the forces applied to a structural member with the internal stresses, strains, and deformations that result — and predicts the point at which it fails. This is the field that sizes the beam, the shaft, and the bolt.

Stress & Strain

UNIFORM AXIAL STRESSσ ACROSS AREA A

When a load is applied to a member, it doesn't just move or hold still — internal forces develop throughout its cross-section to resist it. Stress is that internal force divided by the area it acts on. Strain is the resulting deformation, expressed as a fraction of the original length.

For a slender bar loaded straight along its axis, the stress is assumed to spread evenly across the cross-section — this is the simplest case in the subject, and the one everything else builds on.

Normal Stress
σ = P / A

Axial Loading & Deformation

A rod under axial load doesn't only stress — it stretches or shortens. That change in length, δ, is what a designer actually needs when parts must fit, or when thermal or mounting tolerances matter.

For members with stepped cross-sections or several materials in series, total deformation is just the sum of the deformation in each segment.

Axial Deformation
δ = PL / (AE)
δ elongation (m)   L original length (m)   AE axial stiffness

Torsion

CIRCULAR SHAFT IN TORSIONτ = Tr/J

Twisting a shaft sets up shear stress that's zero at the centerline and maximum at the outer surface — the opposite intuition from axial loading, where stress is uniform. This is why hollow shafts are so efficient: material near the center barely contributes to torsional strength anyway.

Torsional Shear Stress
τ = T·r / J
T torque (N·m)   r radial distance   J polar moment of inertia
Angle of Twist
φ = TL / (JG)
G shear modulus (Pa)   φ twist angle (rad)

For a solid circular section, J = πr⁴/2. For a hollow tube, J = π(ro⁴ − ri⁴)/2.

04

Shear Force & Bending Moment

Before you can size a beam, you need to know how internal shear force and bending moment vary along its length. Shear and moment diagrams plot exactly that — and they're built from a simple relationship: the slope of the moment diagram equals the shear, and the slope of the shear diagram equals the negative of the distributed load.

Load–Shear–Moment Relations
dV/dx = −w(x)    dM/dx = V(x)
V internal shear force   M internal bending moment   w distributed load

Peaks in the moment diagram occur where shear crosses zero — that's where a beam is checked first for bending stress.

Bending Stress

LINEAR STRESS DISTRIBUTIONσ = My/I

A beam in pure bending develops compressive stress on one side of the cross-section and tensile stress on the other, with a linear transition through an unstressed neutral axis. Stress is highest at the outer fibers — which is why I-beams put most of their material as far from the neutral axis as possible.

Flexure Formula
σ = M·y / I
M bending moment   y distance from neutral axis   I moment of inertia
Horizontal Shear Stress in Beams
τ = V·Q / (I·b)
Q first moment of area above the cut   b width at the cut

Deflection

Stress tells you whether a member breaks. Deflection tells you whether it's still usable — a floor beam can be well within its stress limit and still feel unacceptably springy. Deflection is governed by the curvature of the beam, which is proportional to the bending moment.

EI · d²v/dx² = M(x)

07

Combined Loading & Mohr's Circle

PRINCIPAL STRESSESGRAPHICAL TRANSFORMATION

Real members rarely see just one type of load — a shaft can bend and twist at once, a pressure vessel wall carries stress in two directions simultaneously. Stress transformation equations, or their graphical twin, Mohr's circle, find the maximum normal and shear stress at any orientation — the values that actually govern failure.

Principal Stresses
σ1,2 = σavg ± R
R = √[((σx−σy)/2)² + τxy²] — the radius of Mohr's circle

The center of the circle sits at the average normal stress; every point on the circle represents the stress state on some plane through the material.

Under combined loading, "when does it fail" stops having a single stress value as the answer. Failure theories translate a multi-axial stress state into an equivalent stress that can be compared against a material's known uniaxial strength.

09

How to Solve a Mechanics of Materials Problem

Every problem in this subject, no matter how it's dressed up, follows the same sequence: idealize the structure, find what's holding it up, find what's happening inside it, then check that against what the material can take. Work the steps in order — skipping ahead is where most errors come from.

1

Idealize the structure set up

Sketch the member on its own, clean of everything but what affects it mechanically: supports, applied loads, dimensions, and material. Replace real supports with their idealized symbols — pin, roller, fixed — and note what each one restrains.

2

Find the support reactions statics

Draw the free-body diagram of the whole member and apply the equations of equilibrium. If there are more unknown reactions than equilibrium equations, flag the problem as statically indeterminate — you'll need a compatibility equation later, not just statics.

ΣFx = 0   ΣFy = 0   ΣM = 0
3

Cut a section and find internal loadings internal forces

Section the member at the point — or points — you care about. Draw the free-body diagram of one side of the cut and solve for whatever internal loads act there: axial force N, shear V, bending moment M, torque T. For a full picture, build the shear and moment diagrams along the whole length.

4

Identify the critical section where it matters

Internal loadings usually aren't constant along the member. Locate the section where the combination of N, V, M, and T is most severe — often, but not always, where the bending moment is largest.

5

Get the section properties geometry

Compute whatever the stress formulas will need for that cross-section: area A, moment of inertia I, polar moment J, distance to the outer fiber c, and first moment of area Q if you'll need shear stress.

6

Calculate the stresses apply the formulas

Plug the internal loadings and section properties into the matching stress formula. A member under combined loading needs more than one of these, evaluated at the same point.

σ = P/A   σ = My/I   τ = Tr/J   τ = VQ/Ib
7

Combine stresses at the point superposition

If more than one stress acts at the same point, superpose them algebraically, then use stress transformation or Mohr's circle to find the principal stresses and maximum shear stress — the values a failure theory actually needs.

8

Check against strength failure criterion

Compare the governing stress to the material's strength using the failure theory appropriate to the material — Tresca or von Mises for ductile, maximum normal stress for brittle — and apply the required factor of safety.

FS = σ_failure / σ_allowable
9

Check serviceability deflection

Strength isn't the only requirement. If the member also has a stiffness or deflection limit, integrate the curvature equation or use a tabulated case to confirm deflection stays within what's allowed.

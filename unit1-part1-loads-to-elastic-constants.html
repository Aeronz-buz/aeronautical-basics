Loads, stress and strain

    01Loads and their types

    A load is the set of external forces acting on a mechanism or engineering structure, arising from the service conditions in which the component works.

    On an aircraft, "service conditions" is a long list: lift and drag on the wing, thrust from the engines, cabin pressurisation, inertia loads in a manoeuvre, landing impact at the gear, and gusts. Structural analysis begins by classifying each of these by how it is applied relative to the member axis, because that classification decides which internal force appears and therefore which formula applies.

    The five categories

    The line of action coincides with the longitudinal axis of the member, passing through the centroid of the cross-section. It produces a uniform direct stress and pure elongation or shortening — no bending. Tensile loads pull the ends apart; compressive loads push them together. Wing-spar caps, engine-mount struts and control rods are axially loaded members.

    Compression is not just tension with a minus sign

    A short block in compression fails by crushing, but a slender strut fails by buckling — an instability governed by the second moment of area and the end fixity, not by the strength of the material. This is why aircraft structures use stringers and ribs to break long panels into short ones.

    The line of action is perpendicular to the member axis. Transverse load produces bending moment and shear force, so the stress varies across the depth of the section. Three idealisations cover almost every case in this course.

    Converting a distributed load

    For statics only, replace a UDL by a resultant wL acting at mid-span, and a UVL by wL/2 acting at L/3 from the larger end. Use the resultant to find reactions — never to find internal stresses at a section.

    Two equal and opposite forces act parallel to, and very close to, the plane of the resisting area. The material tends to slide along that plane rather than stretch across it. Every riveted and bolted joint in an airframe is a shear-loaded connection.

    A couple applied about the longitudinal axis twists the member. Torsion produces shear stress that is zero at the centre and maximum at the outer fibre, which is why torque-carrying members are hollow tubes — the material near the axis earns very little. The wing torsion box and the engine drive shaft are the obvious examples.

    Real structures rarely see one pure load case. An aircraft wing simultaneously carries bending from lift, torsion from the pitching moment, axial load from the spar caps and shear in the webs. Within the elastic range the individual effects are added by superposition, then resolved into principal stresses to assess failure.

    Why the classification matters in the exam

    Name the load type first and the correct governing equation follows immediately: axial → σ = P/A; bending → σ = My/I; direct shear → τ = P/A; torsion → τ = Tr/J. Most lost marks come from applying one of these to the wrong load type.

    02Stress

    Stress is the force of resistance per unit area offered by a body against deformation.

    When an external load is applied, the body does not simply move away — internal forces develop between adjacent particles to oppose the change in shape. Stress measures the intensity of those internal forces. It is defined on an imaginary cut through the body: expose a section, draw the free body, and the distributed internal force divided by the exposed area is the stress on that plane.

    σ = PA 2.1

    Read the units carefully

    1 Pa = 1 N/m² is a very small stress. Engineering work is done in MPa, and 1 MPa = 1 N/mm² exactly — so if you keep forces in newtons and areas in mm², your answer is already in MPa. Mixing mm with metres is the single most common source of a 10⁶ error in this unit.

    03Strain

    Strain is the ratio of the change in dimension to the original dimension of an object under the application of an external force.

    Stress cannot be measured directly; strain can. A strain gauge bonded to a structure reports the change in length per unit length, and the stress is then inferred through the material law. Because strain is a ratio of two lengths it is dimensionless — and because the numbers are small, it is usually quoted in microstrain (1 µε = 10⁻⁶).

    Sense of a scale

    An aluminium airframe member working at 100 MPa carries a strain of ε = σ/E = 100/73000 ≈ 0.00137, that is 1370 µε — about 1.4 mm of stretch in a metre of material. Structural strains really are that small, which is why "small deformation" assumptions hold throughout this unit.

    04Types of stress

    Only two basic stresses exist: normal stress (σ) and shear stress (τ).

    Every other named stress is one of these two, or a combination. Bending stress is a combination of tensile, compressive and shear stresses. Torsional stress, as encountered in twisting a shaft, is a shearing stress. Recognising this keeps the subject small: there are two stress types and two matching strain types, and everything else is bookkeeping.

    Normal stress — also called linear or direct stress

    If the stress acts normal (perpendicular) to the area concerned, it is a normal stress. It is tensile when it pulls the two faces of the cut apart and compressive when it pushes them together; the universal sign convention takes tension as positive.

    Shear stress

    Where the cross-sectional area of a block of material is subject to a distribution of forces that are parallel, rather than normal, to the area concerned, those forces are associated with a shearing of the material and are called shear forces. The intensity they produce is the shear stress.

    τ = PA 4.1
    P
    total force acting parallel to the area
    A
    area over which it acts

    Single shear and double shear

    A rivet in a simple lap joint is in single shear — one plane resists the load, τ = P/A. The same rivet in a butt joint with two cover plates is in double shear: two planes share the load, so τ = P/2A and the joint carries twice the force for the same stress. Check the number of shear planes before you divide.

    05Types of strain

    Each stress has its matching strain: normal stress produces normal strain, shear stress produces shear strain.

    • Normal strain (ε) — also linear or longitudinal strain. A change of length per unit length. Angles between the faces of an element are unchanged.
    • Shear strain (γ) — a change of angle. It is the angular distortion of the element, measured in radians, and its magnitude is the transverse displacement divided by the distance between the sliding planes.
    • Volumetric strain (εV) — change in volume per unit volume, used with the bulk modulus and equal to the sum of the three linear strains.

    Why γ is in radians

    Shear strain is defined as tan γ, but structural shear strains are so small that tan γ ≈ γ. Keeping γ in radians is what makes the definition G = τ/γ dimensionally consistent, so G comes out in the same units as stress.

    06Hooke's law

    Stress is directly proportional to strain within the elastic limit of the material.

    σ ∝ ε  ⟹  σ = E · ε6.1
    E = σε6.2
    σ
    stress, in Pa (N/m²)
    ε
    strain, no unit
    E
    Young's modulus, the constant of proportionality, in Pa

    Because ε is dimensionless, E carries the units of stress and is numerically very large — 73 GPa for aluminium alloy, 200 GPa for steel. E is a measure of stiffness, not of strength: heat-treating a steel raises its yield strength substantially while leaving E essentially unchanged. A structure that is failing by excessive deflection needs a stiffer material or a better section, not a stronger alloy.

    The three words that matter

    "Within the elastic limit". Every formula in this unit — σ = P/A with δ = PL/AE, the elastic constant relations, superposition of load cases — rests on Hooke's law and is invalid once the material yields. Always check the computed stress against the yield strength before trusting a deflection.

    The single most informative test in materials engineering: pull a specimen to destruction and plot σ against ε.

    08Elastic constants

    Three moduli, one ratio — the four numbers that describe how an isotropic material responds elastically.

    Young's modulus, E — modulus of elasticity

    The ratio of tensile or compressive stress to tensile or compressive strain.

    E = σtεt = σcεc8.1

    Shear modulus, G — modulus of rigidity

    The ratio of shear stress to shear strain. It governs the twist of a torsion box and the distortion of a shear web.

    G = τγ8.2

    Bulk modulus, K

    The ratio of direct (normal) stress to volumetric strain, when the stress is applied equally on all three faces — hydrostatic loading. K measures resistance to a change of volume, where E and G measure resistance to changes of shape.

    K = σεV,  where εV = εx + εy + εz8.3

    Only two are independent

    For an isotropic material, knowing any two of E, G, K, ν fixes the other two through the relations derived in §10–§12. That is the whole point of the next three sections, and it is why the calculator in §12 needs only two inputs.

    09Poisson's ratio

    The ratio of lateral strain to linear (longitudinal) strain.

    ν = Lateral strainLinear strain9.1

    Stretch a bar and it gets thinner; compress it and it bulges. Poisson's ratio quantifies that coupling. It is written as a positive number by convention, with the negative sign of the lateral strain understood, so the strain in a perpendicular direction is −νσ/E.

    0 ≤ ν ≤ 0.5 for any real isotropic material

    At ν = 0.5 the material is incompressible — rubber sits close to it — and equation 11.4 then makes K infinite. Above 0.5, K comes out negative, meaning the material would expand in volume under hydrostatic pressure. That is physically impossible, so a computed ν greater than 0.5 always signals an error in the data or the arithmetic. Keep this in mind when you reach Problem 1.

    10Relation between E and G

    A block in pure shear is also a block in tension and compression along its diagonals. Computing the diagonal strain two different ways gives the relation.

    Step 1 — geometry of the diagonal

    The strain of the diagonal is the extension EC′ divided by the original length AC. Since AE ≈ AC for small distortion:

    Strain on AC = EC′AE = EC′AC1

    From triangle CC′E, the angle CC′E is 45°, so EC′ = CC′ cos 45° = aγ/√2 (using CC′ = aγ from the definition of shear strain). From triangle ACB, AC = AB/cos 45° = a√2.

    Step 2 — the strain in terms of γ

    Strain on AC = aγ√2 × 1a√2 = γ2 = τ2G2

    the last step using γ = τ/G from the definition of the rigidity modulus.

    Step 3 — the same strain from the principal stresses

    Pure shear is statically equivalent to a tensile stress σ₁ = +τ along the diagonal AC and a compressive stress σ₂ = −τ across it. Using the two-dimensional Hooke's law with Poisson coupling:

    Strain on AC = σ₁E − νσ₂E = τE − ν(−τ)E = τE(1 + ν)3

    Step 4 — equate

    τ2G = τE(1 + ν)  ⟹  E = 2G(1 + ν)A

    Sanity check it every time

    With ν between 0 and 0.5, the relation forces G to lie between E/3 and E/2. Steel: 200/2.6 = 77 GPa — matches the tabulated 80 GPa. If your computed G falls outside that window, ν is wrong.

    11Relation between E and K

    Apply equal stress on all three faces, add up the three linear strains, and compare with the definition of K.

    For a general triaxial state, the strain in the x-direction is the direct term less the Poisson contributions from the other two directions:

    εx = σxE − νσyE − νσzE11.1

    For hydrostatic loading σx = σy = σz = σ, so this collapses to

    εx = σE(1 − 2ν)11.2

    By symmetry εy and εz are identical, so the volumetric strain is three times this:

    εV = εx + εy + εz = 3σE(1 − 2ν)11.3

    Substituting into the definition K = σ/εV, the stress cancels:

    E = 3K(1 − 2ν)B

    Where the (1 − 2ν) comes from

    The direct stress stretches the element along x by σ/E; the stresses on the other two faces each pull it back by νσ/E. Three identical terms, two of them opposing — hence the factor (1 − 2ν). Set ν = 0.5 and it vanishes: no volume change is possible, and K → ∞.

    Eliminate ν between relations A and B and the three moduli connect directly.

    From A, ν = E/2G − 1. Substituting this into B and clearing the fractions:
